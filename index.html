Every Region Is Getting Greener — The Difference Is in the Trend.

Satellite data spanning 25 years shows every major region on Earth is getting greener — except the Amazon, whose trend is so weak it barely registers. The world is healing. Earth's most vital forest is standing still.

Shaun Israni Hoang Pham Jared Wang
Chapter 01

Vegetation is changing — and satellites let us see it.

NASA's MODIS instrument has been measuring the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) since 2000, producing a continuous monthly record of plant life across the entire planet. For the first time, we can watch forests, grasslands, and croplands breathe — season by season, year by year.

NDVI measures the ratio of reflected near-infrared to red light — healthy, dense vegetation absorbs red and reflects infrared, yielding values near 1. Bare soil and water hover near 0.

This static NDVI snapshot shows the vegetation density across the Americas for a single month. In later chapters, we explore how this signal changes over time — where regions are greening, where they are browning, and why long-term trends matter more than any single moment.

Chapter 02

Every region is getting greener. The data is unambiguous.

Of six regions tracked across the Americas, four show statistically significant greening (p < 0.05): Canada/Arctic, the Andes, Central America, and the Midwest. The Western US and the Amazon both trend upward on paper — but neither clears the significance threshold. The greening story is real, but it is not universal.

Gaining regions Losing regions
Four of six regions have statistically significant positive trends (p < 0.05). CO₂ fertilization, longer growing seasons at high latitudes, and land-use change all contribute. The Western US and the Amazon are the exceptions — both stressed ecosystems where the signal is too weak to confirm.

Chapter 03

A forest can look healthy. The trend can tell a different story.

NDVI snapshots show the Amazon as one of the greenest places on Earth. But snapshots hide momentum. When we look at the rate of change — the slope — the picture shifts. Most regions are steadily greening. The Amazon is not keeping pace.

Amazon basin · slow growth
High vegetation density can mask weak momentum. The slope — not the snapshot — is what reveals long-term resilience.

Canada/Arctic’s slope is 4.6 times steeper than the Amazon’s — a divergence that grows wider every year. The Amazon stays flat while Canada surges upward.

Sources & Methods

Vegetation data: NASA MODIS Terra Monthly NDVI Level 3, via the NASA GIBS WMS endpoint (gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov), layer MODIS_Terra_L3_NDVI_Monthly. Spatial extent: longitude −170° to −30°, latitude −60° to +75°. Temporal extent: monthly composites, January 2000 through April 2025.

Pre-processing: raw imagery cached, cropped, and converted to numeric arrays. Annual means computed pixel-wise, summarized into analysis regions. Tree-cover percentages derived from MODIS VCF Collection 6 (MOD44B).
