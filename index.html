Every Region Is Getting Greener — The Difference Is in the Trend.

Satellite data spanning 25 years shows every major region on Earth is getting greener — except the Amazon, whose trend is so weak it barely registers. The world is healing. Earth's most vital forest is standing still.

Shaun Israni Hoang Pham Jared Wang
MODIS NDVI · Americas · Jul 2024 · NASA GIBS
Chapter 01 · Step 1

Satellites have been watching the planet breathe since 2000.

NASA's MODIS instrument has been measuring the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) every month since March 2000, producing a continuous record of plant life across the entire planet — forests, grasslands, and croplands alike.

Chapter 01 · Step 2

NDVI is a ratio, not a photo.

NDVI measures the ratio of reflected near-infrared to red light. Healthy, dense vegetation absorbs red and reflects infrared — yielding values near 1. Bare soil and water hover near 0. The color scale below maps yellow-greens to sparse cover, deep greens to dense canopy.

This July 2024 snapshot shows the Americas at peak Northern Hemisphere summer — the Midwest and Canada flush with growth, the Amazon a deep perennial green year-round.
Chapter 01 · Step 3

A snapshot shows density. The trend shows momentum.

High NDVI in a single month tells us vegetation is dense right now. It says nothing about whether that density is growing, stalling, or eroding over years. To understand trajectory, we need to look at the slope of the entire 25-year record.

Regional NDVI trend slope · 2000–2025
Chapter 02 · Step 1

Every region is getting greener.

When we fit a linear trend across all 300 months of NDVI data for each region, every single one shows a positive slope. The planet's vegetation — taken as a whole — is increasing. That story is unambiguous.

All slopes positive
Chapter 02 · Step 2

Four regions clear the significance bar. Two don't.

Statistical significance (p < 0.05) separates confident trends from noise. Canada/Arctic, the Andes, Central America, and the Midwest all clear this threshold. The Western US and the Amazon have positive slopes on paper — but neither is statistically confirmed.

4 significant 2 uncertain
Chapter 02 · Step 3

CO₂ fertilization, longer seasons, land-use change.

Rising CO₂ boosts photosynthesis in most plants. Higher Arctic temperatures extend growing seasons by weeks per decade. Agricultural intensification in the Midwest turns more bare soil to crop canopy. These forces compound — which is why the greening signal is so consistent across regions.

The Western US and the Amazon are the exceptions — both stressed ecosystems where heat stress, drought, and deforestation push back against the global tide.

Every region is greening.
And yet the Amazon — Earth's lungs — barely moves.

Amazon basin · vegetation trend
Chapter 03 · Step 1

A forest can look healthy. The trend can tell a different story.

NDVI snapshots show the Amazon as one of the greenest places on Earth — dense, dark canopy year-round. But a snapshot hides momentum. When we look at the rate of change, the picture shifts.

Amazon basin · slow growth
Chapter 03 · Step 2

High density can mask weak momentum.

The Amazon scores high on NDVI because it has been a forest for millennia — that baseline is baked in. What the satellite can't show in a single frame is whether that canopy is thickening, thinning, or simply holding on.

The slope — not the snapshot — is what reveals long-term resilience.
Chapter 03 · Step 3

Canada's slope is 4.6× steeper than the Amazon's.

Indexed to 2000, Canada/Arctic's NDVI has climbed steeply over 25 years. The Amazon's line is nearly flat. That divergence — and the fact it widens every year — is the central finding of this dataset.

The Amazon's trend is so weak it fails to reach statistical significance. For the world's most important carbon sink, "barely growing" is not reassuring.

Sources & Methods

Vegetation data: NASA MODIS Terra Monthly NDVI Level 3, via the NASA GIBS WMS endpoint (gibs.earthdata.nasa.gov), layer MODIS_Terra_L3_NDVI_Monthly. Spatial extent: longitude −170° to −30°, latitude −60° to +75°. Temporal extent: monthly composites, January 2000 through April 2025.

Pre-processing: raw imagery cached, cropped, and converted to numeric arrays. Annual means computed pixel-wise, summarized into analysis regions. Tree-cover percentages derived from MODIS VCF Collection 6 (MOD44B).
